Taylor's Theorem: An Underdog in Machine Learning¶

How a 300-year-old result quietly powers two of the most important algorithms in modern ML

Introduction¶

Ask any ML practitioner to name the mathematical pillars of their field and you will hear: linear algebra, probability theory, calculus, maybe a touch of information theory. Taylor's theorem rarely makes the list. It sits quietly in undergraduate analysis textbooks, sandwiched between mean value theorems and power series, often dismissed as a tool for approximating sine and cosine. And yet, once you look carefully, it is lurking at the heart of two of the most consequential ideas in machine learning: gradient descent and XGBoost.

This article is a case for giving Taylor's theorem the credit it deserves. We will state it precisely, derive the approximations it enables, and trace exactly how those approximations give rise — with mathematical honesty — to the update rules and objective functions that practitioners use every day.

1. Taylor's Theorem: The Precise Statement¶

Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be $(n+1)$-times continuously differentiable on an open interval containing $a$. Then for any $x$ in that interval:

$$ f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x - a)^k + R_n(x) $$

where the Lagrange remainder is:

$$ R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x - a)^{n+1} $$

for some $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$.

The multivariate extension — what machine learning actually needs — replaces $x \in \mathbb{R}$ with $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^d$. For a loss $\mathcal{L}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ that is twice continuously differentiable, expanding around a point $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) + \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^\top (\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0) + \frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^\top \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0) + \mathcal{O}(\|\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0\|^3) $$

where $\nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the gradient vector and $\mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the Hessian matrix with entries $H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j}$.

This is the master equation. Everything that follows is a consequence of choosing how many terms to keep, and what to do with the result.

The key takeaway: a 1st-order Taylor expansion is just a tangent line. A 2nd-order expansion is a parabola fitted to the local curvature. Gradient descent uses the tangent line. XGBoost uses the parabola. Everything else follows from that choice.

2. Gradient Descent: The First-Order Truncation¶

2.1 The Setup¶

Let $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be a differentiable loss we wish to minimize over $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^d$. We are at current iterate $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$ and want to compute $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}$. Apply the first-order Taylor expansion around $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \approx \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) + \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)^\top (\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_t) $$

This approximation holds when $\|\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_t\|$ is small.

2.2 The Constrained Minimization¶

We want to choose a direction $\boldsymbol{\delta} = \boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_t$ that minimizes the linear approximation subject to a step-size constraint:

$$ \min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \; \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)^\top \boldsymbol{\delta} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|\boldsymbol{\delta}\| \leq \eta $$

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

$$ \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)^\top \boldsymbol{\delta} \;\geq\; -\|\nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)\| \cdot \|\boldsymbol{\delta}\| $$

with equality when $\boldsymbol{\delta} = -\eta \frac{\nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)}{\|\nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)\|}$. Absorbing the normalizing constant into a scalar learning rate $\alpha > 0$:

$$ \boxed{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \alpha \, \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)} $$

This is gradient descent. It is not a heuristic. It is the exact solution to minimizing a first-order Taylor approximation of the loss under a norm constraint.

2.3 What Gets Thrown Away¶

Gradient descent discards the quadratic term:

$$ \frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_t)^\top \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_t) $$

When the loss landscape is highly curved (large eigenvalues of $\mathbf{H}$), this discarded term is large, and gradient descent oscillates or converges slowly. The learning rate $\alpha$ is a proxy for curvature — when tuned correctly, it implicitly compensates for the missing Hessian information.

Visualizing Gradient Descent on a 2D Loss Surface¶

The left panel shows gradient descent on the bowl $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \theta_1^2 + 4\theta_2^2$. This bowl has unequal curvature — it is four times steeper along $\theta_2$ than $\theta_1$, meaning the same step size overshoots badly in one direction while being too cautious in the other. The result is the zigzag path you see.

The right panel shows the 1D mechanism directly. At the current iterate $\theta_t$, the first-order Taylor expansion is a tangent line. Gradient descent steps in the direction this tangent line descends, landing at $\theta_{t+1}$. When the true loss curves sharply, the tangent is a poor approximation of what lies ahead.

The zigzag is not a bug — it is a structural consequence of ignoring the quadratic term. A method that keeps the second-order term (Newton's method) would converge in one step on this bowl, because it accounts for the curvature difference between the two directions. The reason gradient descent remains the default in deep learning is not that it is better — it is that computing the inverse Hessian for a model with millions of parameters is completely infeasible. The learning rate $\alpha$ is a blunt, scalar approximation of what the full Hessian inverse would tell you.

3. XGBoost: The Second-Order Full Treatment¶

3.1 The Boosting Framework¶

XGBoost builds an ensemble of $T$ trees additively:

$$ \hat{y}_i^{(T)} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_t(\mathbf{x}_i), \quad f_t \in \mathcal{F} $$

At iteration $t$, trees $f_1, \ldots, f_{t-1}$ are fixed. We choose $f_t$ to minimize:

$$ \mathcal{L}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\!\left(y_i,\; \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\right) + \Omega(f_t) $$

3.2 Taylor Expansion of the Objective¶

Expand $\ell\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)$ as a second-order Taylor series in $f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)$ around $\hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}$:

$$ \ell\!\left(y_i,\; \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\right) \approx \ell\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}\right) + g_i\, f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{1}{2}\, h_i\, f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)^2 $$

where:

$$ g_i = \frac{\partial\, \ell(y_i, \hat{y})}{\partial \hat{y}}\Bigg|_{\hat{y} = \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}}, \qquad h_i = \frac{\partial^2 \ell(y_i, \hat{y})}{\partial \hat{y}^2}\Bigg|_{\hat{y} = \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}} $$

$g_i$ is the first-order gradient (residual signal); $h_i$ is the second-order gradient (local curvature). Dropping the constant $\ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)})$:

$$ \tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)^2 \right] + \Omega(f_t) $$

3.3 The Regularization Term¶

For a tree with $J$ leaves and weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^J$:

$$ \Omega(f_t) = \gamma J + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j^2 $$

3.4 Solving for Optimal Leaf Weights¶

Let $I_j = \{i : \mathbf{x}_i \text{ falls in leaf } j\}$, $G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i$, $H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i$. The objective decomposes leaf-by-leaf:

$$ \tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \left[ G_j w_j + \frac{1}{2}(H_j + \lambda)\, w_j^2 \right] + \gamma J $$

Each bracket is a univariate quadratic. Setting $\frac{\partial}{\partial w_j}[\cdot] = 0$:

$$ \boxed{w_j^* = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda}} $$

The optimal tree score (minimum loss for a fixed structure):

$$ \tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)*} = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda} + \gamma J $$

3.5 The Split-Finding Criterion¶

When splitting leaf $j$ into left child $L$ and right child $R$:

$$ \text{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma $$

A split is accepted only if $\text{Gain} > 0$. This is not a heuristic — it is the exact improvement in the Taylor-approximated objective minus the complexity penalty.

3.6 Why the Second Order Matters¶

For log loss (binary classification) with $\sigma$ the sigmoid:

$$g_i = \sigma(\hat{y}_i) - y_i \qquad h_i = \sigma(\hat{y}_i)(1 - \sigma(\hat{y}_i))$$

$h_i$ encodes model uncertainty. When the model is already confident ($\hat{p}_i \approx 0$ or $\approx 1$), $h_i$ is small — the leaf update shrinks for already-decided samples. This curvature-aware weighting is invisible to first-order GBDT and is a key source of XGBoost's empirical edge.

Visualizing $g$ and $h$ Under Log Loss¶

Both $g_i$ and $h_i$ are computed per sample at each boosting round. Together they describe how the loss surface looks locally around that sample's current prediction. The left panel shows $g_i$ — the residual between predicted probability and true label. The right panel shows $h_i = \hat{p}_i(1 - \hat{p}_i)$, which is the variance of a Bernoulli random variable with parameter $\hat{p}_i$.

$h_i$ peaks at $\hat{p}_i = 0.5$ — maximum uncertainty — and collapses toward zero as the model becomes confident in either direction. Now look at the optimal leaf weight: $w_j^* = -G_j / (H_j + \lambda)$. The denominator $H_j$ is the sum of $h_i$ values across all samples in that leaf. When a leaf is populated with samples the model is already confident about, $H_j$ is small and $w_j^*$ shrinks — the tree contributes less to those predictions. When a leaf contains genuinely uncertain samples, $H_j$ is large and the update is more aggressive.

This is automatic, loss-derived regularization. Vanilla gradient boosting, which only uses $g_i$, has no mechanism for this — it treats all samples in a leaf as equally worthy of correction regardless of how certain the model already is.

4. A Unified View¶

Both algorithms are Taylor series truncations applied to optimization:

Gradient Descent XGBoost
Expansion point Current params $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$ Current predictions $\hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}$
Variable expanded Loss w.r.t. parameters Loss w.r.t. predictions
Order kept 1st (gradient only) 2nd (gradient + Hessian)
Optimization Step in $-\nabla$ direction Closed-form quadratic min
Curvature handling Implicit via $\alpha$ Explicit via $h_i$

Keeping the second-order term turns an unconstrained direction problem into a quadratic with a closed-form solution. It also makes the algorithm loss-agnostic: any twice-differentiable $\ell$ plugs in unchanged — just swap $g_i$ and $h_i$.

5. Conclusion¶

Taylor's theorem is not merely an approximation tool from undergraduate calculus. It is the unifying mathematical language of iterative optimization. Gradient descent is what you get when you keep one term and trust the geometry. XGBoost is what you get when you keep two terms and solve exactly. The difference in terms is the difference between a direction and an answer — and it explains, in large part, why XGBoost swept tabular ML competitions for a decade.

The next time you tune a learning rate or watch XGBoost split a node, you are watching Taylor's theorem run in production.
